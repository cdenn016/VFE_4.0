S1.1 The conditional relative-entropy identity

The disintegrations used here are regular conditional laws on standard Borel spaces [6].

Let µ,  be probability laws on a product X × Y of standard Borel spaces. Write their marginals as
µY , Y and their conditional laws as µy, y. Then

DKL(µ) = DKL(µY Y ) + DKL(µyy)µY (dy).                                            (S1.1)

The identity holds in [0, ], both sides being infinite together. Suppose first µ   and put
f = dµ/d, a jointly measurable function. Then a(y) = f (x, y)y(dx) is a version of dµY /dY , and
b = f /a on {a > 0} is jointly measurable with µy = b(·, y)y for µY -almost every y. Starting from
f in this way avoids choosing a jointly measurable version of the conditional densities separately.

The logarithm splits into log a + log b. The negative parts of x log x are bounded by 1/e, so the

negative parts of the two logarithms have finite µ-integrals and the split integrates in [0, ], giving
eq. (S1.1). If µ is not absolutely continuous with respect to , then either µY   Y or µy   y on a
set of positive µY -measure, and both sides are infinite. This argument underlies the predictive and
reconstruction identities in the main text.

S1.4 A shared source and two routes

Let X be a uniform bit. Two routes return Y1 = X and Y2 = X  b, where b  {0, 1} is a fixed route
mark and  is addition modulo two. The comparison O = Y1  Y2 equals b with probability one. If
the second route instead uses an independent uniform bit X, the same comparison is uniform and
reveals no route mark. The marginal law arriving on each route is uniform in both experiments.
Thus the shared-source dependence, not those marginals, determines the comparison outcome. A
record model intended to inspect a loop must specify which occurrences refer to the same source.
In general, a functional of the pair of route laws cannot distinguish two loop maps H, H with the
same pushforward of the source law, whereas the joint law of a shared sample and its image along
the loop determines the loop map almost everywhere; the route mark is the simplest instance.

S1.5 Predictive sufficiency for a selected response

Let X = (A, B) be two independent uniform bits, let the encoder retain U = A, and take no side
information. For target Y = A, one has I(Y ; X | U ) = 0. For target Y = B, the same representation
gives I(Y ; X | U ) = log 2. Perfect predictive retention is therefore a statement about a specified
target. If a later interaction reads B, the first sufficiency statement does not supply its response.
This elementary distinction persists when X is a complete configuration of law-valued agents and
Y is a boundary process.

This chapter gives supporting matrix identities for interactions written in a reference vector space.
All spaces are finite dimensional and real. The results concern arbitrary finite matrices and do not
require Gaussian laws, diagonalizability, or invertibility of the generator. A statistical interpretation
enters only when the matrices are assigned to interfaces or made functions of law parameters.

Gaussian comparison costs and
repeated variables

This chapter distinguishes a Gaussian comparison likelihood from a divergence between Gaussian
laws by direct calculation. The comparison likelihood is normalized in its record. Its expectation
depends on the joint law of the contents that enter the comparison, including whether two ap-
pearances denote the same variable. The calculations require finite second moments; Gaussian
recognition is needed only where a Gaussian relative entropy is evaluated.

This chapter gives two exact ways to determine what a parent must retain: factorization of a
boundary map and conditional prediction from a retained history. Both distinguish an inherited
response from a newly chosen parent interaction.

S4.1 Linear boundary descent

Proposition S4.1 (Descent through a surjective retention). Let Ej, Ei, Zj, Zi be finite-

dimensional real vector spaces. Let Cj : Ej  Zj be surjective, let Ci : Ei  Zi be linear,
and let Mij : Ej  Ei be a linear boundary map. There is a linear map MIcJ : Zj  Zi satisfying

                Ci  Mij = MIcJ  Cj ,   (S4.1)

if and only if

                ker Cj  ker(Ci  Mij).  (S4.2)

When it exists, it is unique.

Proof. If (S4.1) holds, every v  ker Cj satisfies Ci(Mijv) = 0. Conversely, define MIcJ (Cjv) =
Ci(Mijv). Two choices v, w with Cjv = Cjw differ by an element of ker Cj, which (S4.2) annihilates.
The definition is therefore independent of the representative. It is linear, and surjectivity of Cj
defines it everywhere. The same surjectivity proves uniqueness. 

This is the factor theorem for linear maps. It covers deterministic linear retentions only. For a
stochastic sample retention the parent channel must be a Markov kernel, and its existence is the
garbling condition stated with the channel square in the main text; a linear solution with negative
entries does not qualify.

For example, let Cj(u, v) = u and Ci be the identity on R. The map Mij(u, v) = u + v cannot
descend: the discarded vector (0, 1) changes the retained output. The map Mij(u, v) = 2u
descends to multiplication by two. Agreement of two current input laws does not change either
conclusion, since the requirement concerns the boundary mechanism on its declared domain.

The same criterion handles an additive-noise channel without requiring Gaussian noise. Suppose
the receiver content is Mijv + , where  has a fixed probability law  independent of v. If (S4.2)
holds, its retained law is the distribution of

S4.2 Exact finite filtering

Let Xn be a Markov chain on a finite configuration space S with transition matrix T and initial row
distribution p0. Let Zn = R(Xn) for a map into a finite retained space Sc. For each z, let Pz be the

diagonal matrix whose entry at x is 1{R(x)=z}. These matrices are selectors of configurations, not
new dynamical transitions.

history hn = (z0, . . . , zn) of positive probability, define

fhn = p0Pz0 T Pz1 · · · T Pzn ,                           phn  =  fhn .  (S4.4)
                                                                  fhn 1

Then phn is the conditional distribution of Xn given hn, and

Pr(Zn+1 = z | hn) = phn T Pz1.                                           (S4.5)

After a positive-probability next value z, the update is

                             p(hn,z)          =  phn T Pz .              (S4.6)
                                                 phn T Pz1

For any finite future word, its probability is obtained by further multiplication by the corresponding
T Pz matrices and by 1. The recursion is the forward algorithm for a function of a Markov chain
[1, 4]. The normalized vector phn is itself a Markov process on the simplex of laws on S, moving to
(S4.6) with probability (S4.5).

Proof. The x component of p0Pz0 is the joint probability of X0 = x and Z0 = z0. Multiplication
by T propagates this joint mass one step; multiplication by Pz1 restricts it to the next retained
value. Induction gives the joint mass of hn and Xn = x as the x component of fhn. Its sum is
the probability of hn, so division gives the conditional distribution. One further propagation and
selection gives (S4.5) and (S4.6). Repeating the operation gives every future word probability. 

A zero-probability history has zero unnormalized message and no conditional law supplied by this
formula. Because all matrices are nonnegative, every extension of that history also has zero mass.
The construction therefore needs no arbitrary prediction on an impossible history.

The filter is sufficient even when the retained process is not Markov. For the deterministic cycle
1  2  3  1 with uniform initial law, set R(1) = R(2) = 0 and R(3) = 1. The histories (0, 0) and
(1, 0) both have positive probability and end at the same retained value. The first ends at state 2
and predicts 1 next; the second ends at state 1 and predicts 0. Hence the current retained value
cannot determine its next distribution. The terminal conditional law does: it is the point mass 2
after (0, 0) and 1 after (1, 0). Here the filter recovers the fine state, so it compresses nothing; a
history-based parent forgets information only when noise or nonobservability prevents the history
from determining the fine state.

S4.3 Successive retention of responses

Suppose a declared joint law contains a fine state X and a boundary response O. Let Z = R10(X)
and W = R21(Z), and assume all spaces are standard Borel. For every bounded measurable
response observable g, conditional expectation gives

E[g(O) | W ] = E E[g(O) | Z] | W .                (S4.7)

To verify the identity, integrate either side over any event determined by W . Such an event is also
determined by Z, so both integrals equal the integral of g(O) over that event. This characterizes
conditional expectation up to null sets.

The identity concerns one declared joint law. Applying it to an equilibrium law produces equilibrium-
conditional responses. Applying it to the joint law conditional on a retained history produces the
corresponding predictive responses. These are interchangeable only when their relevant conditional
laws agree. Exact boundary descent is stronger: it fixes the response on every admitted fiber,
independently of the conditional weights used there.
