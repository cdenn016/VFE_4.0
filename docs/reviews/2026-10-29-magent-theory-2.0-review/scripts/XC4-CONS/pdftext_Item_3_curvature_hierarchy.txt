Proposition 4.3 (Flat wall walks and a common boundary dictionary). Consider two clusters
I, J whose internal dictionaries are identity. Assume that bridges exist in both directions. For every
bridge e : j  i from J to I and every bridge g : i  j from I to J, require directed internal paths
i  i in I and j  j in J. Internal strong connectivity is sufficient. Cross-scale edges may be
included only when they supply the required directed paths. Let the bridge dictionaries be arbitrary
elements of a group G.

Every directed walk that uses e, such internal paths, and g has holonomy ¯g¯e. All these walks are
flat exactly when there is one ¯D  G such that all bridges J  I carry ¯D and all bridges I  J
carry ¯D-1. At first order the corresponding condition is e = D, g = -D.

For rigid clusters with common operating content c and zero bridge records, if the free bridge
dictionaries satisfy proposition 3.1, the linearized wall loop acts on c by (Fe - Fg)(yI - yJ ). More
generally, with identity internal dictionaries but nonzero internal disagreements, that action is

                                  (e + g)c = (Fe - Fg)e + Fg(e + g).                                                   (4.6)

Proof. The internal factors are identity, so the ordered product is ¯g¯e. The path hypothesis realizes
every opposite bridge pair as a walk of this kind. Fix one bridge in each direction, which is possible
by nonemptiness. The equations ¯g¯e = I for all pairs imply that every forward bridge is the inverse
of the fixed backward bridge, and every backward bridge is the inverse of that common forward
value. The converse follows by multiplication. Replacing products by their first-order sums gives
the generator condition. The stationary bridge equations give ec + gc = Fee + Fgg, which is
eq. (4.6); rigidity makes g = -e.

Without the path hypothesis, separate reciprocal bridge pairs can each be flat while carrying
different dictionaries. For I = {a1, a2} and J = {b1, b2} with no internal directed paths, let b1  a1
carry scalar dictionaries 1, 1 and b2  a2 carry 2, 1/2. Every available directed loop is flat, but the
bridges from J to I disagree. Parent-to-child edges alone supply no return paths between these
pairs.

When the proposition applies, all records in one bridge direction read a common dictionary through
their own codes. Grounded referents with positive-definite second moment identify it up to the
intersection of those code stabilizers; an injective stacked code identifies it completely for the
location-family experiments. The group-level flatness statement is independent of Gaussianity.
Its stationary gain formula is a Gaussian tangent statement. Neither licenses replacing distinct
dictionaries by one merely because a single-topic experiment fails to distinguish them.

Remark 4.4 (Repeating a supplied rigid restriction). The density factorization can be repeated
along a supplied hierarchy whenever the required measures and normalizers exist. In the Gaussian
family the parent self term pools child information through eq. (4.4), while the bridge factors retain
the boundary records. At finite coupling, integrating out children instead gives a Schur complement;
representing it within the prescribed comparison-record family is an additional closure requirement.

These statements do not select a partition, prove its persistence, or construct an inherited response
and update kernel for a predeclared probe class. They also do not establish compression on a
post-handoff invariant set, interaction necessity against a null flow, or a second selection depending
on the first. Those are separate requirements of the formation standard. A supplied rigid parent
density therefore does not by itself establish recursive formation or complete dynamical inheritance.

Remaining extensions

Open problem 5.1 (Formation with top-down feedback). Determine whether a specified dynam-
ics can select persistent blocks and create parents whose feedback supports further selection. The
construction must retain the laws, records, evaluators, and complete response and update data
required by its admitted protocols, and must meet the compression, null-flow, and counterfactual-
recursion conditions. The supplied partitions and minimizer limits above do not settle these
questions.

Open problem 5.2 (Fluctuations beyond the fixed tangent experiment). In the present tangent

model CP is dictionary independent, so its determinant contributes no dictionary force. For a

nonlinear link model with Gaussian conditional content precision J(), information vector b(),

and  scalar  energy  term  k(),  Gaussian  integration  gives  k()  -  1  b()T  J ()-1b()  +  1  log  det  J ()
                                                                       2                      2

up to a constant, provided J() is positive definite. Determine the complete action in a declared

nonlinear communication model, including its off-topic directions and the signs of the resulting loop

terms. The quadratic penalty of theorem 2.2 alone does not supply that calculation.

Open problem 5.3 (General content laws). Identify hypotheses under which the evidence action
for general laws admits a useful decomposition into absorbable reinterpretations and residual
incompatibility. Such a result must preserve record dependence and distinguish an invariance of the
likelihood from an invariance of the content measure. Establishing a normalized soft-to-rigid limit
also requires more than the finite product normalizers used for the static restriction. The stationary
rank-one and gain formulas are not claimed outside the Gaussian tangent family.

Open problem 5.4 (Learning gains from loop records). For a declared grounded or relay
experiment, determine which combinations of code, noise, and dictionary-anchor strength can be
inferred from observed loop effects. The gain-weighted formula is an identity at a stationary tangent
state; an identifiability theorem requires the full admitted record experiment and cannot be inferred
from that identity alone.
