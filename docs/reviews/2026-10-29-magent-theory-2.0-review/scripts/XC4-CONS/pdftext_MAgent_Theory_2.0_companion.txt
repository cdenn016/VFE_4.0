Chapter 1

What an Agent Carries

A classical statistical description distinguishes a phase-space point from a distribution over phase
space. The same distinction begins this theory: k  K is a represented possibility, while q is a
probability law over such possibilities. The agent carries the law and mechanisms for changing
it. Its contents need not be positions and momenta, and the statistical law need not describe an
ensemble of physically separate agents.

1.1 Beliefs, models, and predictions

There are two sectors. The belief law qi describes state contents k. The model law si describes
model presentations m  M. Their most general joint description is

i(dk, dm) = qi(dk | m)si(dm).         (1.1)

The notation qi(dk | m) means a conditional law, and qi(dk) without the condition denotes its
marginal. Choosing i = qi  si removes dependence between the sectors in that description. It
still permits coupled equations for qi and si, just as coupled equations for two coordinates do not
require a statistical ensemble over those coordinates.

A model presentation becomes predictive through a normalized evaluator Gi(m, dk). For each m,
this assigns a probability law to the state contents. Averaging the evaluator over the model law
gives

(siGi)(A) = Gi(m, A)si(dm).           (1.2)

For example, let k, m be binary, with si(m = 1) = 0.6, Gi(1, {1}) = 0.9, and Gi(0, {1}) = 0.2. The
model mixture assigns probability 0.62 to k = 1. A present belief qi(k = 1) = 0.8 is a different object.
An interaction rule can bring these descriptions into agreement, revise the model law, or change
both. Those dynamics must be specified.

An evaluator is a Markov kernel: a function whose output is a probability measure. Ordinary
functions are included as the case in which the output measure is concentrated at one value. This
language accommodates discrete, continuous, singular, and multimodal laws without selecting a
Gaussian family. When differentiating laws later, we will restrict to a regular statistical family or
positive finite tables.

1.2 A belief is not a generative model

The evaluator is half of a generative model. The other half is a record likelihood i(k, m; do), which
says how represented contents would show up in the agent's own registers. Together with a prior

ri(dm) over presentations they form one normalized joint law,

                          Pi(dm, dk, do) = ri(dm)Gi(m, dk)i(k, m; do).  (1.3)

Each presentation m labels a complete forward model: a prior over contents and a likelihood for
records. The agent's laws qi and si are something else. They are its current beliefs, the recognition
law that approximates the posterior Pi(dm, dk | o) once a record o is in hand, and their quality is
scored by the variational free energy DKL(iPi(· | o)) - log Pi(o). The analogy is a detector with a
calibration curve. The calibration curve is the generative model; the current estimate of the signal
is the belief. Two detectors of one design share the calibration curve and can still hold different
estimates; two detectors with the same estimate predict the same counts only if their calibrations
also agree.

The same distinction organizes populations. Agents whose generative models agree up to a rela-
beling of presentations and contents form a species. Sharing a model is a fact about mechanisms.
Agreement of beliefs is a fact about states, and a record registered by one member changes its
beliefs without changing those of a member that did not register it.

Without records the best belief under a model is the prior itself, with the conditional qi(dk | m)
equal to Gi(m, dk). Under the product restriction i = qi  si this is no longer reachable: the best
belief at fixed si is the geometric average qi  exp log gi(m, ·)si(dm) of the evaluator densities,
not the arithmetic mixture siGi. The product description therefore already separates what an agent
believes from what its models predict.

1.3 A record has an address inside the agent

Let xi  Si denote the complete internal state, including its laws, interfaces, and any memory. A
record readout is an ordinary measurable function

                          i : Si - Oi.                                  (1.4)

The output alphabet Oi specifies the distinctions the agent can read. A binary register gives
Oi = {0, 1}; a real-valued comparison can use Rr. The detector analogy is precise at this level: an
internal state contains a readable distinction. It does not require an outside observer to inspect it. A
detector reading must also not depend on the coordinates in which it is written down. A readout of
the first coordinate of a displayed mean would change under a mere rotation of the agent's frame,
so readouts are required to be invariant, or to transform by a declared rule, under such passive
changes.

Write x  S for the complete population configuration. A deterministic interaction Fij : S  S

produces the record

                          oij = i pri Fij(x) ,                          (1.5)

where pri selects the receiver's state. For a stochastic transition ij(x, dx), the probability of a

record in A is

                          Rij(x, A) = ij x, {x : i(pri x)  A} .         (1.6)

This is a pushforward: collect the probability of every next state that displays an outcome in A. The
transition specifies what happens; the readout specifies what is accessible. A persistent record
requires that some later readout still recover the distinction. A transient comparison and a memory
trace therefore have different dynamical requirements.

A likelihood on represented contents has another role. For instance, the expression o = Aiki -
Bijkj +  gives a comparison value once the contents and noise are given. Their joint law predicts
possible comparisons. A registration rule must still place the outcome in an internal state and
specify the response to it.

1.4 Which probability space is being used?

The configuration x contains the agents' laws. A tuple of represented contents (ki, mi)i belongs to
a different space. A probability law over possible configurations is a third construction, useful when
a preparation or stochastic dynamics has been declared. Deterministic motion of x requires neither
sampling the contents nor drawing an ensemble of configurations at each step.

This distinction also clarifies correlations. Two independent fair bits have probability 1/4 for each pair.
Condition on a shared record that the bits agree. Their marginals remain fair, but the conditional
joint law has probability 1/2 on each of (0, 0) and (1, 1). A calculation of the next joint comparison
needs this dependence; the marginals do not contain it. A coupled product-family evolution remains
a possible model, but it will not represent that exact conditional joint law. It does not even keep the
fair marginals. The best product description of the hard agreement record is a point mass on (0, 0)
or on (1, 1). For a soft record  eJ12 in spin variables, the symmetric product description is the
mean-field magnet m = tanh(Jm): unmagnetized for J  1 and spontaneously magnetized above
J = 1, although the exact posterior keeps zero magnetization for every J. Whether a correlation is
needed is decided by the response being predicted.

A third kind of law joins the contents of several agents, or of one agent at several contexts, into
one joint law of fields. Only such a law can predict a record that compares the same sample along
two routes. A comparison of the two route laws cannot see a loop that leaves the transported law
unchanged, such as a rotation of an isotropic Gaussian, whereas the joint law of a sample and its
image along the loop determines the loop map.

The laws can be attached to local domains of a context base C. This is the section picture: a
context selects the law applicable there, and a frame specifies its coordinates. Most interactions
below occur at a common context. Moving between contexts introduces the connection problem in
chapter 3.

Frames, Messages, and Interactions

An agent's probability law and the coordinates used to describe that law are different objects.
Communication introduces a third object: a rule for translating, filtering, or modifying what another
agent supplies. Keeping these distinctions explicit prevents a coordinate change from being
mistaken for an interaction, or a selective message from being mistaken for an invertible transport.

2.1 Reading a link from source to receiver

There are two sectors. Write iq = qi for the belief marginal and is = si for the model marginal. In a
finite-dimensional realization, a sector a  {q, s} has a reference vector space Va, an agent space
Eia, and an invertible presentation

Uia : Va - Eia,             ¯ia = (Uia)-#1ia.        (2.1)

The pushforward notation expresses the represented possibilities in reference coordinates. The
space Eia is agent i's displayed copy of Va: at one context all agents' laws lie in one fiber of the
statistical bundle, and the presentations are frames used to display them. The two sectors carry
two representations of one frame group, with different dimensions, laws, and interaction rules: a
belief vector rotates by q(g) and a model presentation by s(g) under the same change of frame
g. Think of one set of axes carried by the agent, with beliefs and models as two different kinds of
object written on those axes. Each edge can carry a belief link and a model link, both valued in
the same group. When they agree the edge transports beliefs and models the same way; when
they differ, the mismatch ~iji-j1 is a property of the edge that no change of frame removes. The
collective geometry of chapter 3 uses this single group.

A law-dependent reference generator and its displayed link are

Laij = Laij (¯ia, ¯ja),     iaj = UiaeLaij (Uja)-1.  (2.2)

The index order is receiver first, source second. Reading from right to left, the source is expressed
in reference coordinates, the generator acts there, and the result is expressed at the receiver. The
function Laij is a constitutive choice. The exponential parameterization does not derive it or equate
the belief and model generators. It is also only one kind of interaction. The general interaction is a
normalized Markov channel, possibly noisy or information-discarding; the exponential link is the
special deterministic channel y  aijy. Its values eL lie in exp(gl(Va)), which in dimension at least

two misses some determinant-one matrices, such as -1 1 , and every reflection [12].

2.2 Changing coordinates

For a passive change yi = giayi, both the law and its presentation change: ia = (gia)#ia and
Uia = giaUia. The reference law stays fixed, giving

                                           iaj = giaaij (gja)-1.                              (2.3)

This endpoint covariance is familiar from gauge link variables [50], here in display coordinates. It
holds for every generator rule, because the reference laws do not change, and so it constrains
nothing: the frames act as a dressing field [15]. Every interaction cost can be computed entirely in
reference coordinates. Metrics and additional model data must also be re-expressed when forming
scalar predictions. Covariance alone supplies no physical symmetry or evolution law.

Changing the common reference presentation is a separate operation. Under Uia  Uiaha, the
reference laws become (h-a 1)#¯ia. Reference consistency requires the constitutive rule, including
any interface data, to give Liaj = h-a 1Laijha. The identity eha-1Lha = h-a 1eLha then leaves the
displayed link unchanged. This is the substantive symmetry requirement. It is not automatic, and
the main text imposes it as an explicit standing hypothesis wherever a conclusion should not depend
on the choice of reference frame.

2.3 Selective interfaces and an explicit pair

Sending and receiving can be represented by

     Tja : Va - Ha,                        Oia : Ha - Va,               Laij = OiaTja.        (2.4)

can lose dimensions. Its exponential is always invertible and acts as identity on ker Tja. Thus
exponentiating a low-rank generator retains unread directions, and the link compares complete
laws. Sending only the extracted message is a different, information-discarding operation. General
normalized Markov kernels allow communication without any linear realization.

3.8 Geometric structure and physical interpretation

The pullback construction is already a mathematical geometry of contextual statistical change. Its
physical interpretation requires a model of the contexts, a mechanism selecting the comparison
data, and observable responses to transport. Inherited evolution and inherited responses impose
further conditions on a parent; connection preservation is an additional requirement.

Fisher geometry is positive semidefinite. An indefinite metric can be introduced through a separate
construction. For example, a Hermitian 2 × 2 matrix H = tI + x ·  has determinant t2 - |x|2,
where  are the Pauli matrices. Pulling its determinant bilinear form back by an invertible soldering
field, and dividing by a positive scale that transforms as | det g|2, gives a Lorentzian metric invariant
under the full complex frame group. The natural scale is the determinant of the aggregate second
moment; the metric is then one half of a complex-Gaussian Fisher metric minus the square of the
entropy rate, so time-like directions are those in which the entropy changes faster than the law's
Fisher speed. Without a scale the form is invariant only under frames with | det g| = 1. A circle
extension then requires a circle connection and a positive radius. Those choices do not follow from
Fisher positivity, and their existence does not establish spacetime dynamics.

The dynamical variables are probability laws. Moving an agent therefore means changing its
expectations, uncertainties, and dependencies, rather than moving a point through its sample
space. The physical analogue developed here is overdamped motion on a probability manifold: an
objective supplies the driving covector, and the Fisher metric converts that covector into a velocity.
Neither ingredient determines the other.

4.1 Three objectives

Before any equation, one must say what is being minimized, and for interacting agents there are
three different answers. In the global picture all agents are parts of one normalized joint law over
their contents and records, as the spins of one lattice share one Boltzmann weight. Each agent's
own free energy is then that single functional seen as a function of its own factor, peers act on it only
through expectations of their contents, and every local update lowers the same total: coordinate
descent, like a sequence of single-spin heat-bath moves.
In the per-agent picture each agent has its own generative model, in which its own evaluator and
the laws its peers communicate compete as sources of prior. The peers' laws are data for the agent,
like an external field it does not control. Each agent lowers its own free energy, but the free energies
of the agents that listen to it change through terms it does not control. Such non-reciprocal forces
need have no global Lyapunov function: on a directed ring of three Bernoulli agents, each pulled
toward its successor, the linearized motion spirals, with complex eigenvalues that no gradient flow
can have.
The third object, developed in this chapter, is the reciprocal law potential. It adds the divergences
between agent laws directly, so each edge pulls its receiver toward the source and also pulls the
source toward its receivers, like a spring that acts on both ends. All agents then descend one
shared function, and the dynamics dissipates that function and cannot spiral into an equilibrium.
The price is that this function is not the free energy of any fixed normalized model in which the agent
laws are the beliefs, and records enter it only as separate assimilation events. The constructions of
the later chapters use this third object; their conclusions are not claims about the other two.

5.9 Compression should name what matters

Rate­distortion theory makes the desired fidelity part of the communication problem [44]. A low-
dimensional real vector alone is not a finite-bit channel. Noise, precision, or an encoding constraint
must specify the communication cost. Wyner­Ziv coding additionally gives the receiver a realized
variable correlated with the source [51]; possession of a prior by itself is not that realized side
information.

For a predictive criterion, name the source X, target response Y , and accessible receiver informa-
tion Z. An encoder generates U from X alone, so U - X - (Y, Z) is a conditional-independence
chain. The optimal decoder is P (Y | U, Z). Its expected relative-entropy distortion is

D = I(Y ; X | U, Z) = I(Y ; X | Z) - I(Y ; U | Z).  (5.42)

The equality follows by expanding I(Y ; X, U | Z) in both orders. It is the information about
the chosen response that compression discards. An information-bottleneck criterion trades this
quantity against a cost such as I(X; U | Z) [48]; an operational bit rate requires additional coding
assumptions.

If X = (A, B) consists of independent fair bits and U = A, the distortion is zero for target Y = A
and log 2 for target Y = B. For a prospective parent, the target can be a future boundary response.

When a Collective Can Act as an Agent

A collective can have a simple description without being able to evolve from that description
alone. Knowing the total energy of two bodies, for example, does not usually determine their
next interaction. A parent of law-valued agents faces the same question: does its retained state
determine its future responses?

There are two operations to distinguish. A channel C compresses represented contents and gives a
parent law QxC. A map R retains a complete dynamical state R(x). Besides belief and model laws,
that state can include the model evaluator, source information, boundary channels, and memory. A
distribution over possible contents does not automatically contain these additional data.

6.5 What a parent must remember

When closure fails, a parent can retain more state or, under a declared stochastic process, the
conditional law of hidden states given its accessible history. In a finite model this is a probability
vector: propagate it with the fine transition, restrict to the new retained value, and normalize. That
vector determines every future response even when the current summary does not. Keeping only
an equilibrium hidden distribution generally changes transient predictions. The vector lives on the
simplex of all fine states, so it saves nothing unless noise or poor observability keeps the history
from pinning down the fine state.

There is a middle case in which the parent needs no memory although the current summary is
not autonomous for every preparation. Suppose that for each retained value z there is a fixed way
(z, ·) of spreading probability over the fine states with that value, and that spreading and then
evolving gives the same result as evolving the parent and then spreading, T = T c. A child
population prepared by spreading a parent law in this way stays spread in this way forever: the
hidden distribution is always (Zn, ·), and the parent is an ordinary Markov chain with kernel T c
[42]. A handoff that prepares children differently can break the parent, even though the kernels are
unchanged.

Given the fine update and a list of what the parent must reproduce (its laws, the responses
to a declared set of probes, and the channels it reads), there is a smallest exact parent. Two
configurations are lumped together exactly when the listed quantities agree for them now and at
every later time. Nothing coarser can be exact, and anything exact remembers at least this much. A
longer list of probes gives a finer parent; if outside agents may probe the children without restriction,
they can tell every exposed child law apart and the parent cannot forget any of them. How much a
parent forgets is therefore decided by the probes declared before the parent is chosen.

6.6 The formation standard

The theory aims to show that interacting agents can produce further agents. A construction that
groups agents and hands their laws to parents does not yet show this, because the grouping and
the handoff can be arranged without the interaction doing anything. The formation standard of the
main text lists six clauses, each closing one such loophole.

The first clause asks for a set of starting configurations that is open, or a family declared in advance
and preserved by the dynamics. A single trajectory is not enough. Along one path, almost any
summary of the state traces out a curve, and every curve is the solution of some differential equation,
so "the parent evolves autonomously" would hold for nearly any choice of parent. Exactness means
something only when it is tested on configurations that the parent cannot tell apart. The second
clause asks the dynamics to pick nontrivial groups that persist by a declared criterion, with the
moment of handoff read off from the state or its accessible history. The third asks that each group
carry inherited laws, evaluators, responses, and dynamics, with the probes that outsiders may use
fixed before the parent is chosen.

The fourth clause is compression. A parent that simply keeps every child's state is always exact
and says nothing. Four agents with Gaussian laws pulled together by symmetric peer costs make
the point: a threshold rule first pairs the two closest agents, then the other two, then the two pairs,
and keeping the full tuple of child states at each stage reproduces the dynamics exactly. Nothing

                                          34
6. When a Collective Can Act as an Agent      A Physicist's Companion

has been forgotten, so nothing has formed. The clause therefore asks each parent to lump together
configurations that some admitted probe of the children could still distinguish.

The fifth clause is the control experiment. Delete every cost that couples one agent to another,
freeze the population dependence of the channels at its initial value, keep everything else, and
run the same selector. This is the null flow. If it produces the same groups, the interaction did no
selection work. Agents that do not interact can be grouped by any rule that reads their initial data
and a common relaxation clock, much as runners in a race can be grouped by finishing order even
though they never touch. The standard asks that the sequence of selected groups differ from the
null one on an open set of starting configurations. Only the groups count: if the interaction merely
makes the same joins happen sooner or later, it has changed a clock, not the outcome. A stronger
version asks that the final grouping not be predictable from any summary of the initial data that
evolves the same way with and without interaction, and an amplifying version asks that groups be
tighter relative to their separations than they would be without interaction.

A whole class of architectures fails this control. If every receiver compares itself with the same
target law, and the channels see the population only through additive moments, then each pair of
agents relaxes toward each other at a common rate: the logarithm of the ratio of their laws shrinks
by the factor e-2t, up to a constant. Without the peer costs the same quantity shrinks by e-t. The
interaction then only speeds up a clock that every pair shares, and any selector that compares
pairs in this way produces the same sequence of groups with or without it, at different times. The
same happens whenever summing two agents' parameters is an exact parent for every pair: the
parameter equation must then be affine, all differences shrink by a common factor, and the order of
joins is fixed by the initial distances. Selection that depends on the interaction needs receivers that
weigh different sources differently.

The sixth clause is recursion. The parents must themselves be grouped, by the inherited interactions,
without resetting their data or adding a new attraction tuned for the purpose, and this second
grouping must depend on what the first stage produced. If it would occur from every possible
handoff in the inherited class, the second stage is a sequence of scales supplied in the initial data,
not a parent built from parents.

A construction that meets the selection, inheritance, and recursion clauses but fails compression or
the null-flow control is called a two-stage selection with exact inheritance. The constructions in the
later chapters are measured against this standard. The nonsmooth fusion example meets the null-
flow control, since without coupling its agents never collide, at the cost of changing the interaction.
The common-precision matrix-law construction of chapter 11 selects two parents and then their
union, but its schedule does not depend on the coupling: it is a two-stage selection with exact
inheritance. The six-agent construction of chapter 12 works on a full open neighborhood of laws
with selective channels, but in the regime of its proof the null flow reproduces its membership history.
Both smooth constructions supply their compression architectures in advance. A construction that
meets every clause, with a selector and a compression interface derived rather than declared,
remains open.

original smooth flow. Each belief and model law has a public bit, a private bit, and an internal gate
bit. A fixed normalized local evaluator couples the public belief and model means. The gate means
relax toward a fixed reference value. The private part of a peer channel depends on the source's
private mean multiplied by a smooth function  of the population's gate means.

The function  has an exactly flat zero region. Its derivative is included in the Fisher equations.
The gate is in effect an internal clock: it relaxes on its own, perturbed only by a term that a small
private gain keeps dominated, and it enters the flat region at a finite time and stays there. Once
inside, every peer's private cost in a receiver row is the same for all sources. It cancels from the
Gibbs source comparison. The reduced energy and Fisher metric then split into a public part and
an independent private-and-gate part. Both the finite-time erasure and the change of preferred
source follow from a declared ordering of the private gain, the public peer strength, and the gate
scale; the example does not show that the KL dynamics produces either without that ordering.

The public states start in two invariant sign regions, with two agents in each. No labels are assigned
to groups by the equations. Initially, private similarities can select crossed pairs, each containing
one member from each public region. After private erasure, the preferred sources change: each
agent prefers the other member of its public region, and that preference persists. All cross-pair
source weights remain positive. The two members of a preferred pair are distinct at every finite time,
but this heterogeneity is transient: for a fixed temperature and a small enough public peer strength,
their public states converge exponentially to each other, so each pair approaches a common state.

The interaction is not what produces this sequence. If every peer cost is deleted, the same rule for
choosing preferred sources reads the crossed pairs first and the regional pairs afterward, because
the private advantage and the public sign regions are supplied by the initial states; only the moment
of the switch moves, since the gates then close more slowly. The example is therefore a single-stage
selection with exact inheritance, not a formation.

For each pair, retain the empirical measure of its two paired public belief and model means. The
original public vector field gives its exact evolution, and fresh public responses are computed by
selecting and sampling a child from that measure. The private and gate laws can be forgotten
after erasure, because they form a factor of the dynamics that has decoupled and that no admitted
response reads. This is a noninjective reduction, but the empirical measure keeps every public
child state, up to its label, and it is not itself a law pair of the children's type.

The example is an existence construction with an engineered channel, a product preparation
on finite alphabets, and a specified class of boundary responses. It adds no fusion penalty and
imposes no synchronization. It does not show that arbitrary smooth channels acquire a sufficient
interface, that the distinct public child states admit a smaller aggregate description, or that the two
resulting groups form another parent. Those are the remaining formation obligations.

12.3 What switching off the interaction does

The formation standard asks whether a grouping would also occur with the interaction switched
off. Deleting every peer cost leaves u = -u, so u(t) = e-tu(0), and the rows become a Gaussian
kernel of growing width applied to the initial positions. Integrations from the proof's initial data, at

not the exact field at the theorem's temperature, and they are not a theorem. What is proved is that
peer forces contract the pairs faster than the null flow does. The groups themselves are fixed by
the scale hierarchy written into the initial data.

The groups are also temporary. Every mean tends to zero, so every row tends to 1/6. A first-stage
contrast tends to 1/6 - 4/6 < 0, and once only the merged core and the third pair remain, the
contrast condition has no exterior and the two remaining rows tend to 2/6 and 4/6. The same
rule therefore merges everything at a later finite time. The windows in the theorem are finite by
construction.

12.5 Which responses survive

Before any selection, declare that a query to a current block returns the bit vector and the sum
modulo 2 of independent constituent angles. A model query uses the analogous product of model
laws. The same rule applies at every block size. Native source choices and transmitted bits are
also available, and future query choices may depend on the entire recorded history.

The sum of canonicalizing rotations is zero, so the response distribution calculated from eq. (12.13)
is exactly the original one. It has a nontrivial angular signal: its first moment is the product of the
constituent first moments. At the next join, bit vectors concatenate and angular sums add.

Equality of the waiting-time flow and the next joint response kernel proves equality of every finite
adaptive history by conditioning successively on its records. This concerns the full joint history, not
just its one-time marginals. Earlier finite sample records are carried forward. Positivity means that
they do not exactly reconstruct the discarded continuous offsets. They do carry information about
them: each angle sample of a singleton carries Fisher information equal to its rotation inertia, so
many pre-merge samples estimate the offsets well. The loss is that no finite record set pins them
down exactly. An exact archive of the child laws would remove the advertised loss.

Individual absolute-angle probes of a child inside a compressed block are not allowed by this
interface after the merge. They change under a discarded relative rotation and cannot be recov-
ered. The construction therefore preserves specified experiments, not every possible microscopic
measurement.

12.6 Selection and inheritance are independent

Nothing in the last two sections used the selected groups. The rows and event times are unchanged
by independent rotations; the slice, the moving flow, the response descent, and the geometric
equality below hold for any grouping at any time, including at the start. The bits choose the groups
and the times. The response rule decides that, from those times on, only angle sums are asked for.
The orientation information lost at a handoff is exactly what the coarsened interface stops asking
about. This is why the theorem is stated as a conjunction of selection, interface coarsening, and
symmetry reduction.

16.7 What remains to be selected

Conjugation cannot reveal a continuous gauge subgroup that fixes every input link. If a nonzero

A receiver can infer more variables than a message directly describes because its own model
relates the measured and unmeasured variables. That additional structure belongs to the receiver's
model. It need not reproduce the sender's structure. A second uncertainty concerns alignment:
even when the agents use content spaces of the same dimension, the receiver must learn how
the sender uses those coordinates. A code describes what is expressed, a dictionary describes
the proposed alignment, and a conditional law describes how a receiver reconstructs what was left
unsaid.

All comparisons here occur at one context. There is one principal frame group G, acting on belief
and model contents through two representations q and s. The matrix examples use the belief
representation V = RK and suppress its sector superscript. The model representation can have
a different dimension. One frame change acts in both representations, but a shared dictionary
between the sectors is an additional tied-link assumption. This common group does not make the
two laws or their communication rules identical.
